# Water treatment works operating manual

This manual describes the day-to-day operation of the town's water treatment works and its distribution network. It
is written for shift operators, the duty manager, and contractors who work on site. It does not replace the formal
permits issued by the drinking water regulator; where the two disagree, the permit wins and the operator informs the
duty manager.

## Raw water and the intake

The works draws its raw water from the reservoir behind the dam, about two kilometres upstream of the treatment
building. The intake tower has three draw-off levels, so that operators can choose the water layer with the best
quality at any time of year. In winter the whole water column is mixed by wind and cold, and the upper draw-off is
normally used because it is closest to the surface and easiest to maintain. In summer the reservoir stratifies: a warm
upper layer sits on top of cold, dense water, and the two barely mix until the autumn storms arrive.

Each draw-off has a coarse bar screen followed by a band screen with a six-millimetre mesh. Leaves, twigs and the
occasional fish collect on the screens, and the pumps start to cavitate if the screens clog. The control system
measures the water level in front of and behind the band screens and raises an alarm when the difference grows.
Operators clean the band screens by hand whenever that difference exceeds fifteen centimetres, and in any case once a
week. The debris goes into the skip by the intake gate and never back into the reservoir.

The raw water is sampled at the intake every morning. The laboratory measures colour, turbidity, pH, temperature,
manganese and algae counts, and the results appear on the shift screen before noon. A sudden rise in colour after
heavy rain is normal and is handled by the coagulant dose; a steady rise in manganese in late summer usually means that
the deep water has lost its oxygen, and the upper draw-off is kept in service for as long as the algae allow.

Algae are the main seasonal problem. When the laboratory reports a bloom near the surface, typically in July or August
after a calm, sunny week, the operators switch the works to the lower draw-off at a depth of twelve metres, below the
warm layer where the algae grow. The lower draw-off brings water that is colder and clearer but may carry dissolved
manganese, so the chemical dosing is adjusted at the same time and the laboratory samples the treated water twice as
often for the next three days. The switch is always logged, with the time and the algae count that caused it, and it
is reversed only when two consecutive counts are back below the alert level.

Access to the intake tower is by the footbridge. Nobody crosses the footbridge alone, and the tower is never entered
when the reservoir is above the spillway crest, because the lower gallery may then flood without warning. Life jackets
hang in the cabinet at the bridge end and are worn by everyone on the bridge, including visitors.

## Treatment process

Raw water enters the works through the flow meter and the inlet valve, where the coagulant is dosed. The works uses a
ferric coagulant, which makes fine particles and dissolved colour clump together into flocs. The dose is set by the
jar test that the laboratory runs each morning, and the operators adjust it through the day when the colour or
turbidity of the raw water changes. An overdose is expensive and leaves iron in the water; an underdose lets colour and
particles pass through the filters. After dosing, the water flows through the flocculation tanks, where slow paddles
give the flocs time to grow without breaking them apart.

The flocculated water passes to the clarifiers, where most of the flocs settle out as sludge. The sludge is drawn off
continuously, thickened, and pressed into cake, which a contractor collects every week and spreads on farmland under a
separate permit. The clarifier surface is inspected every shift: a blanket of flocs rising towards the weirs is the
first sign that the dose is wrong or the flow is too high, and the operator reduces the flow before the filters are
overloaded.

The rapid gravity filters come next. There are eight filters, each a concrete box with a bed of sand over gravel, and
the water trickles down through the sand, which catches the remaining particles. As the sand collects dirt, the water
needs more pressure to pass through, and the level above the sand slowly rises. Dirty filters are cleaned by backwashing,
and the clock sets the minimum pace: the duty operator backwashes a filter after 72 hours of filter run time, even if it
still looks clean, because bacteria grow in a bed that is left alone too long. Operators watch the filters on the
control screen, where each filter shows its run time since the last clean and its head loss, the difference in water
level between the top of the sand and the outlet. Backwashing works like this: air and then clean water are blown upwards through the bed, the sand lifts and scrubs itself, and the dirty water is sent to the washwater tank.
Only one filter may be backwashed at a time, because the washwater pumps cannot serve two, and a filter that has just
been cleaned runs to waste for ten minutes before its water rejoins the treated flow. The clock rule is the one most often forgotten on busy night shifts, which is why
the control system flags a filter in orange once it passes 70 hours. Backwashing uses a lot of treated water, so the
operator checks the level of the clear water tank before starting and delays the backwash if the tank is below its
morning level. Backwashing during the evening demand peak is avoided for the same reason. Dirty water from the
washwater tank settles for several hours before the clear water on top is returned to the start of the works, and the
settled solids join the clarifier sludge. The second trigger does not wait for the clock: a filter is also backwashed
as soon as its head loss reaches 1.8 metres, because above that value the bed starts to push dirt through into the
treated water. If more than two filters reach either trigger in the same shift, the operator informs the duty manager,
because it usually means that the clarifiers are carrying over flocs.

After filtration, the water is disinfected with chlorine in the contact tank, which gives the chlorine at least thirty
minutes to act before the water leaves the works. The chlorine dose is controlled automatically from an analyser at
the tank outlet. The target free chlorine residual leaving the works is 0.5 milligrams per litre, which is enough to
protect the water on its way through the network while keeping the taste acceptable. A residual below 0.3 or above 0.8
raises an alarm on the shift screen, and the operator checks the analyser against a hand test before changing the
dose. Finally, the pH is corrected with lime so that the water does not attack the lead and copper pipes in older
houses.

## Distribution and service reservoirs

From the works, the treated water is pumped into the distribution network, which serves about forty thousand people
through three hundred kilometres of pipe. Five service reservoirs on the hills around the town store treated water and
keep the pressure steady; at night they fill, and during the day they supply the demand peaks. Each reservoir has a
level sensor, and the pumps at the works are started and stopped from those levels.

The network is divided into pressure zones. The old town in the valley is fed by gravity from the two lowest
reservoirs, while the newer estates on the ridge need a booster station that lifts the water into a small tower. Each
zone is separated from its neighbours by closed boundary valves, which are painted red on the network maps and must
never be opened without the network supervisor's approval, because opening one can reverse the flow in a main and stir
up years of sediment. Pressure loggers at the far end of each zone send a reading every fifteen minutes; a slow fall in
the night-time pressure is often the first sign of a new leak long before anyone sees water on the road.

Leak detection is a permanent task. The night flow into each district, measured between two and four in the morning
when almost nobody uses water, is compared with the expected minimum for the number of houses in that district. A
district whose night flow rises by more than a tenth over a month is surveyed with listening sticks and correlators,
usually by the two-person leak team on a quiet night. Found leaks are repaired in order of size, except that any leak
under a road, a railway or a building is repaired first, whatever its size, because the water can wash out the ground
beneath it.

The reservoirs are the network's safety margin. If the works has to stop, for example after a power cut or a pollution
alarm on the reservoir, the stored water keeps the town supplied while the problem is fixed. For that reason no service
reservoir may be drawn down below 40 percent of its capacity in normal operation. If a reservoir falls below that
level, the operator starts the standby pump at the works and reports the event to the duty manager, who decides whether
customers need to be asked to save water. In a hot, dry week the reservoirs are refilled overnight to 95 percent, so
that the afternoon peak can be met from storage.

Operators should know how the reservoirs behave on the hottest days. Demand on a summer evening can reach twice the
winter average, mostly from garden watering between six and nine o'clock, and the works alone cannot keep up with it.
The stored water covers the gap, and the reservoirs refill after midnight. A hosepipe ban is the last resort and is
decided by the utility's board, never by the shift.

Every reservoir is inspected inside once a year. The tank is drained, the walls and floor are cleaned with a high
pressure washer, and the hatches, vents and overflow screens are checked for gaps through which birds, insects or
rainwater could enter. The tank is disinfected with a strong chlorine solution before it is filled again, and it goes
back into service only after two clear bacteriological samples taken twenty-four hours apart.

Hydrants are flushed twice a year, in spring and in autumn, district by district. Flushing removes the loose sediment
that settles in the mains when the flow is low, and it tests that every hydrant still works for the fire brigade.
Customers in the district are warned by a leaflet a week in advance, because the water can turn brown for an hour or
two after flushing.

## Incidents and notification

Most alarms at the works are routine and are handled by the shift operator. A small number of events, however, can
affect the safety of the drinking water and must be escalated at once. The shift operator does not wait to be sure;
reporting an event that turns out to be harmless is always acceptable.

Routine alarms include a pump that trips and restarts, a chemical tank that reaches its reorder level, a door contact on
a remote reservoir, or an analyser that drifts and needs recalibration. The operator acknowledges each of them on the
shift screen, notes the cause in the shift log, and clears it once it is resolved. An alarm that repeats more than three
times in a shift is no longer routine, even if each occurrence is harmless, and is reported to the duty manager at the
morning handover so that maintenance can look for the underlying fault.

Power cuts are frequent in winter storms. The works has a standby generator that starts automatically and carries the
treatment process, the chemical dosing and one high-lift pump; it does not carry the full pumping load. During a long
power cut the operator therefore reduces the output of the works to what the generator can support and lets the service
reservoirs cover the difference, watching their levels closely.

The most important of these events is a rise in turbidity at the works outlet. Turbidity is the cloudiness of the
water, and in treated water it is a warning that particles, and possibly pathogens, are passing the filters. The outlet
turbidity is measured continuously. A reading above 1 NTU at the works outlet for more than fifteen minutes starts the
incident procedure: the operator informs the duty manager by telephone, isolates the filter or filters that are most
likely responsible, and increases the chlorine dose within the permitted range while the laboratory takes samples.

The duty manager then decides, together with the health authority, whether customers must be told to boil their water.
That decision must be taken within two hours of the incident being declared, even if the cause is not yet known,
because a delayed notice cannot be taken back. If a boil water notice is issued, it is announced on local radio, on the
utility's website, and by door-to-door leaflets in the affected districts, and bottled water is offered to vulnerable
customers on the priority list.

A pollution alarm on the reservoir is handled differently from a treatment failure. If the ranger service reports an oil
sheen, a dead animal near the intake, or an agricultural spill in one of the feeder streams, the operator closes the
affected draw-off, switches to another level if one is clean, and asks the laboratory for an emergency sample. If no
draw-off can be trusted, the works is stopped and the town is supplied from the service reservoirs while the duty
manager arranges further action.

After every incident, the duty manager writes a report for the regulator. The report records the timeline, the
readings, the decisions taken and who took them, and the changes proposed to prevent a repeat. The regulator receives a
copy.

## Laboratory and sampling

The works laboratory is small but accredited, and its results are the evidence the regulator relies on. Samples are
taken from fixed taps at the intake, after the clarifiers, after each filter, at the works outlet, at every service
reservoir and at forty customer taps spread across the town. Customer taps are chosen at random each month from a list
of volunteer households and public buildings such as schools and the library. The sampler runs the tap for two minutes,
flames the outlet, and fills sterile bottles without touching the inside of the cap.

Bacteriological samples must reach the laboratory within six hours of being taken and are kept in a cool box on the way.
A sample that arrives late or warm is discarded and taken again the same week, because an invalid sample counts as a
missed sample in the regulator's statistics. The laboratory analyses coliform bacteria and E. coli in every sample;
any positive result is reported to the duty manager immediately, and a repeat sample is taken from the same tap and from
the nearest reservoir within twenty-four hours.

Chemical parameters are measured on a rolling programme. Iron, manganese and aluminium are measured daily at the works
outlet, because they come from the treatment process itself. Lead is measured at customer taps in the old town twice a
year, since that is where lead service pipes remain; households with a lead pipe are offered a free replacement of the
part on their own land. Pesticides and other trace organic substances are sent to an external laboratory four times a
year, and the result sheets are filed with the monthly report.

The laboratory also runs the jar tests that set the coagulant dose, and it calibrates the online analysers every week
against its own bench instruments. An analyser that differs from the bench result by more than ten percent is taken out
of automatic control until it has been serviced, and the operator doses by hand from the laboratory readings in the
meantime.
