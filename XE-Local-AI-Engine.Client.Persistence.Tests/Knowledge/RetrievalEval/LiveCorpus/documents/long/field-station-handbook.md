# Coastal field station handbook

This handbook is for everyone who spends more than one night at the coastal research station. Read it before you
arrive; the station manager will assume that you have.

## Arrival and induction

The supply boat lands at the north jetty on Tuesdays and Fridays when the swell allows it. On arrival every visitor
reports to the station manager, hands over any medication that needs refrigeration, and signs the visitor book with
the planned departure date. The induction walk takes about forty minutes and covers the fire exits, the muster point
by the flagpole, the first-aid room and the boot wash. Nobody leaves the station perimeter before finishing the
induction, not even for a short walk along the beach.

Mobile phone coverage is weak and disappears completely in the east wing. The station network has a guest wireless
network whose password is written on the board in the common room; please do not stream video, because the satellite
link is shared by everyone and costs the programme by the megabyte.

## Accommodation

Bunk rooms sleep four. Bedding is provided, but bring your own towel. Quiet hours run from 22:00 to 06:30, and the
drying room is not a place to store food, because the heat attracts mice. Rooms are inspected every Sunday afternoon
and any room that fails inspection loses its weekend beer ration, which has proven to be an effective incentive.

## Power, fuel and communications

The station runs on two diesel generators that take turns week by week, with a battery bank that carries the base
load at night so that the generators can rest for six hours. The duty technician checks the oil level of the running
generator every morning before breakfast and writes the reading into the generator log, together with the engine
hours, the day tank level and any warning light seen on the control panel. The day tank is topped up from the bulk
tank by the transfer pump; never run the transfer pump unattended, because an overflow spills diesel onto the gravel
and into the drain that leads to the bay. Spill kits hang by the door of the generator shed and at the bulk tank,
and every spill, however small, is reported to the station manager on the same day. Solar panels on the workshop
roof contribute a useful share in summer but almost nothing between November and February, so do not plan
experiments that need extra power in winter without talking to the duty technician first. Heavy consumers such as
the kiln in the geology lab or the freezer array in the biology lab are scheduled on the whiteboard in the generator
shed, and only one of them may start at a time, because the inrush current of both together trips the main breaker.
Radio discipline matters as much as power discipline. Every field party carries a handheld radio and checks in with
the base at each full hour. For emergencies the station answers to the call sign Kestrel Base on the primary marine
channel, and that call sign is used by every party, even when the caller knows the operator personally. Keep
messages short, repeat grid references twice, and wait for the read-back before you move on. A party that misses two
check-ins in a row triggers the overdue procedure, which starts with a call to the other field parties and ends,
after ninety minutes, with the search team leaving the station. The radio room is staffed from 07:00 to 21:00; outside
those hours the handheld on the manager's desk is monitored instead, and it wakes the manager with a loud alarm tone.
If the primary channel is blocked by interference or by a stuck transmitter, switch to the backup frequency of
8.291 MHz, which is monitored around the clock by the regional coastguard. The satellite telephone in the radio room
is the last resort and is tested every Monday at noon.

## Water

Drinking water comes from the small desalination unit behind the kitchen. The pre-filters are changed every two
weeks and the membrane after every 900 operating hours, which the unit counts itself. Showers are limited to four
minutes, and laundry is done on Wednesdays and Saturdays only, so that the tank never runs dry during a busy week.

## Waste

Everything that is brought to the station leaves it again. Food waste goes into the sealed bins by the kitchen door
and is burned in the incinerator twice a week. The ash, glass, metal and all hazardous waste are packed in labelled
drums and shipped out on the annual resupply voyage in March. Batteries are never burned.

## Field travel

Travel on the sea ice is only allowed after the ice has been measured and approved by the station manager. A party
of two on foot needs at least ten centimetres of clear ice; a quad bike needs thirty centimetres, and the tracked
vehicle is not taken onto the ice at all. Every party leaves a route card on the board before leaving and wipes it off
on return.

## Medical

The station has no doctor. Two members of the permanent staff are trained in wilderness first aid, and a doctor on the
mainland can be reached by satellite telephone at any time. The medical cabinet is locked; the key hangs in the radio
room. Anybody who takes medication from the cabinet writes it into the medical log, including painkillers.
